Today's lesson

Concept

Stagflation and the Central Bank Dilemma

In standard macroeconomic cycles, inflation occurs when the economy is booming, and slowing growth cools price pressures down. Central banks manage this by raising interest rates to curb inflation or cutting rates to stimulate output. Stagflation breaks this machinery.

It happens when supply-side shocks—such as Brent crude surging to $110 per barrel—drive up production costs while economic output slumps. Today, the IMF projects Bangladesh's growth at just 3.2% with inflation at 10.9%. If Bangladesh Bank cuts interest rates to support economic growth, cheaper credit fuels higher inflation.

If it raises rates to fight price spikes, debt-servicing costs climb, further crushing business investment. The central bank is trapped with no easy lever.

Jargon decoder

These terms appear in today's brief. Hover any dotted word above to see its meaning without scrolling back.

Current account deficit

A situation where a country spends more foreign currency on imported goods, services, and transfers than it earns from exports and remittances.

Crucial today because crude oil touching $110 per barrel inflates import spending, widening this external trade shortfall.

Think of it as a national overdraft caused by buying more abroad than you sell.

Letter of credit (LC)

A formal guarantee issued by a bank promising to pay a foreign seller once imported goods are verified and shipped.

September import LCs jumped 22%, showing that costlier energy is locking up substantial bank dollar liquidity.

An LC is a bank's financial passport that lets goods leave foreign docks.

Stagflation

An economic trap where economic growth stalls and unemployment rises while price inflation remains stubbornly high.

The IMF downgraded Bangladesh's FY27 growth to 3.2% while forecasting inflation at 10.9%, the classic definition of stagflation.

Stagnant growth plus persistent inflation equals stagflation.

Debt service

The total cash required over a given period to pay back loan principal and accrued interest to lenders.

Surging external debt service payments are draining Bangladesh's foreign exchange holdings faster than new loans arrive.

Debt service is the mandatory monthly installment on past borrowing.
