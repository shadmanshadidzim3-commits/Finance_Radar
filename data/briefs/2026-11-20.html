Today's lesson

Concept

The Impossible Trinity

The Impossible Trinity (or the Trilemma) is a central bank's biggest headache. It states that a country cannot have all three of these at the same time: 1) A fixed exchange rate, 2) Free movement of money in and out of the country, and 3) An independent monetary policy (the ability to set its own interest rates). Today, Bangladesh is trying to manage its interest rates to fight inflation while also trying to keep the taka stable.

However, money is leaking out of the system (capital flight). By moving to a 'fully floating exchange rate,' Bangladesh Bank is essentially giving up on the fixed rate to regain control over its monetary policy and stop the drain on its reserves.

Capital shortfall

The amount of money a bank is missing to meet the minimum safety levels required by the government.

Twenty-two banks were reported to have a combined shortfall exceeding Tk 3 lakh crore.

Think of it as a car's oil level; if it's below the minimum, the whole engine risks seizing up.

Floating exchange rate

A system where the value of a currency is decided by supply and demand in the market, not by the government.

Bangladesh Bank is planning to let the taka float fully within a month to satisfy IMF conditions.

In a floating system, the market is the boss; in a fixed system, the central bank is the boss.

Moral hazard

When someone takes risks because they know they won't have to pay for the consequences if things go wrong.

Allowing loan defaulters to access fresh stimulus money encourages them to keep defaulting.

It's like giving a driver a new car every time they crash; they'll never learn to drive safely.

Input cost inflation

When the price of the raw materials needed to make a product goes up.

Cotton and natural gas prices spiked today, making it more expensive to run factories and make clothes.

If the flour gets expensive, the baker has to either charge more for bread or lose money.
