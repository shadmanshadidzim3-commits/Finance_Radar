## Top 10 stories

**1. Fifteen banks hold 85% of Bangladesh's Tk 600,000 crore in non-performing loans.**

The central bank reports that asset-quality distress is concentrated in just 15 lenders. These non-performing loans (NPLs)—loans where the borrower has stopped making payments—trap capital that should be used for new business lending. This concentration creates systemic risk, meaning the failure of one of these large, interconnected banks could destabilise the entire economy.

**2. Government audit reveals GDP growth was overstated by 1.3 percentage points since 2015.**

A forensic audit—an intense investigation of financial records—found that flawed industrial data led to inflated Gross Domestic Product (GDP) figures. GDP measures the total value of goods and services produced. This overstatement means critical ratios like debt-to-GDP are actually worse than previously reported, which could lower the country's international credit rating.

**3. Apparel exports to the European Union fell 12% due to industrial unrest.**

The EU is Bangladesh's largest export destination for Ready-Made Garments (RMG). A double-digit decline in export volume directly reduces the inflow of foreign exchange. This puts pressure on the current account, which tracks the country's trade balance and determines if we have enough dollars to pay for imports.

**4. US 10-year Treasury yield hits 5.125% as the dollar index strengthens to 103.74.**

The US 10-year yield is the global risk-free rate; when it rises, investors pull money out of emerging markets like Bangladesh to earn safer returns in the US. This triggers capital flight and strengthens the US Dollar Index, which measures the dollar against other major currencies. A stronger dollar makes Bangladesh's imports more expensive and increases the cost of servicing foreign debt.

**5. Global cotton prices surged 5.31% in a single day to 81.09 cents per pound.**

Cotton is the primary raw material for the RMG sector. Since Bangladesh imports almost all its cotton, this price spike causes input cost inflation. If manufacturers cannot pass these costs to buyers, they face margin compression—a reduction in the percentage of profit earned on each sale.

**6. Bangladesh Bank sold $100 million from reserves to stabilise the exchange rate.**

The central bank uses reserve auctions to provide liquidity—available cash—to commercial banks struggling to settle import letters of credit (LCs). While this prevents a sharp taka depreciation, it reduces the total foreign exchange reserves. Reserves are the country's ultimate safety net for meeting international payment obligations.

**7. Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant began trial supply of 350MW to the national grid.**

This is the first time nuclear power has been connected to the Bangladesh grid. Adding base-load power—electricity that is consistently available 24/7—reduces the need for expensive, oil-fired 'rental' power plants. This could eventually lower the government's energy subsidy bill and improve industrial productivity.

**8. Government plans debut international sovereign bond issue for early January.**

A sovereign bond is a debt security issued by a national government. By tapping international capital markets, Bangladesh aims to diversify its funding sources and ease the pressure on domestic banks. However, the success of the issue depends on investor confidence in the country's newly audited GDP and fiscal health.

The delay in setting a new minimum wage prolongs industrial uncertainty. Factory owners have threatened 'no work, no pay' shutdowns, which could further disrupt production. Labour stability is a prerequisite for maintaining the export order book, as buyers may shift orders to competitors like Vietnam or India if strikes persist.
