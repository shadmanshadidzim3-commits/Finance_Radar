## The day in one read

Today was characterized by a sobering quantification of Bangladesh's external debt burden. The dominant force was the Economic Relations Division's disclosure that foreign debt repayments are now doubling new loan inflows. This 'negative net inflow' is a structural alarm bell; it means the country is effectively exporting capital to its creditors at a time when its own foreign exchange reserves are under pressure from a 14% spike in natural gas prices.

What changed versus recent days is the shift from 'energy price anxiety' to 'debt servicing reality.' While previous notes focused on the cost of buying fuel, today's data shows the cost of our past borrowing is now the primary drain on dollars. The government's request for $1 billion in Korean budget support confirms that organic inflows like exports and remittances are currently insufficient to cover both the import bill and the debt schedule.

What to watch next is the central bank's response to the City Group debt restructuring request. If the legal exemptions are granted, it signals that the banking regulator is prioritizing the survival of 'too big to fail' conglomerates over the enforcement of single-borrower exposure limits. Watch the USD/BDT median; if it stays below 124.00 despite these pressures, it confirms the central bank is still aggressively intervening in the market.

## Top 10 stories

**1. Foreign debt repayments more than doubled loan inflows in the first quarter of FY27.**

This creates a negative net inflow—where more cash leaves the country than enters. According to TBS, debt servicing hit $1,351 million while disbursements fell 42% to $662 million, putting immense pressure on foreign exchange reserves, the total stock of foreign currency held by the central bank.

**2. Banks seek legal exemptions to lend more to City Group despite its Tk 26,000 crore debt.**

This highlights a concentration risk, where a bank's exposure to a single borrower is so high that the borrower's failure could destabilize the lender. Of the Tk 26,000 crore, Tk 16,246 crore are funded loans—actual cash handed over—which directly impacts bank liquidity if not repaid.

**3. Government seeks $1 billion in budget support from South Korea to manage energy shocks.**

Budget support is a type of foreign aid that goes directly into the government's treasury rather than a specific project. This is a critical fiscal tool to cover the deficit—the gap between what the government spends and what it earns in taxes—amid rising global energy costs.

**4. Global natural gas prices surged 14.47% in a single day to $3.02.**

Bangladesh relies on imported LNG to fuel power plants and factories. A spike in natural gas prices increases the subsidy bill—the money the government pays to keep electricity prices lower for the public—and worsens the trade deficit.

**5. Labor federations demand Tk 37,125 as minimum monthly wage for garment workers.**

The minimum wage is the lowest legal salary an employer can pay. If granted, this would significantly increase input costs for RMG exporters, potentially leading to margin compression—a reduction in the percentage of profit earned on each shirt or pair of pants sold.

This policy aims to improve dollar liquidity within the private sector. By allowing firms to hold foreign currency, the central bank reduces the need for these firms to buy dollars from the market for their own import needs, potentially stabilizing the exchange rate.

State-owned commercial banks (SOCBs) often struggle with high non-performing loans (NPLs)—loans that are not being repaid. Bringing in private sector management is an attempt to improve corporate governance and credit risk assessment in these troubled institutions.
