## The day in one read

Today was a financing day, not a trading day. Nothing dramatic happened on the DSE — the index drifted up on earnings optimism and thin small-cap turnover — but underneath, Bangladesh's external accounts told a harder story. Foreign loan commitments fell to a 14-year low while debt repayment hit a record $4.49 billion. Remittances hit an eight-year high but still can't outrun import demand. Reserves sit at $31 billion after another Asian Clearing Union payment. None of these are shocks on their own; together, they are the financing gap that has been pushing the taka to 122.74 all week.

What is new versus the last two days is that the story has widened from energy to financing. Yesterday it was Brent and LNG cargo costs; the day before it was the rate cut itself. Today confirms the same mechanism from a different angle — Bangladesh Bank cut its policy rate to 9.5% just as the US 10-year yield climbed to 4.745%, narrowing the cushion that used to attract capital into taka assets. Add record debt repayment against collapsing new aid, and every channel that could bring dollars in is either shrinking or barely keeping pace.

Watch USD/BDT against the informal cap Bangladesh Bank imposed this week — if the kerb premium keeps widening while the official rate holds, that is capitulation building, not stability. The number that will confirm or kill this thesis is the next official net reserves print against the $30 billion line.

## Top 10 stories

**1. Bangladesh's foreign loan commitments plunge 37% to a 14-year low of $5.24bn in FY26, while annual debt repayment hits a record $4.49bn.**

Falling loan commitments and record debt service mean Bangladesh's net foreign financing — money borrowed minus money repaid — is shrinking fast, just as the current account already runs a deficit from high fuel and food imports. That leaves fewer dollars to offset the import bill, adding pressure on foreign exchange reserves and the taka. This tightens the balance of payments, the ledger of everything Bangladesh pays and receives from abroad, at exactly the moment energy and remittance flows are already strained.

**2. July remittances hit an eight-year high of $2.85bn, up roughly 15% year-on-year, but stay below the $3bn threshold needed to offset rising import demand.**

Remittances are Bangladesh's largest source of foreign currency after exports, and they flow through formal banking channels only when the official exchange rate stays close to the informal 'hundi' rate. July's inflow is real support for reserves, but import letters of credit are growing faster, meaning net dollar demand still exceeds supply. That imbalance is the direct mechanical driver of the taka's slide to 122.74 this week.

<sub>FX · 🇧🇩 direct · confidence: high · The Daily Star, The Business Standard · [read](https://www.thedailystar.net/business/news/july-remittances-hit-8-year-high-285b-4238356)</sub>

**3. Bangladesh's net foreign exchange reserves stand at $31bn after settling the latest Asian Clearing Union (ACU) payment.**

Bangladesh settles its two-month import bill with other Asian Clearing Union members in a lump sum, and this payment is one of the periodic drains on reserves. A $31 billion net reserve level gives a few months of import cover, the standard yardstick the IMF and rating agencies use to judge reserve adequacy. Every ACU cycle that draws reserves down without an offsetting inflow tightens that cover further.

**4. The US 10-year Treasury yield climbs to 4.745%, up 7.4% over the past month, even as Bangladesh Bank cuts its policy rate to 9.5%.** `US10Y`

When the US 10-year Treasury yield — the benchmark 'risk-free' return for global investors — rises while Bangladesh Bank cuts its own policy rate, the extra compensation investors get for holding taka assets over dollar assets shrinks. That discourages foreign portfolio inflows and encourages capital to sit in dollars, a direct headwind to the taka. It also raises Bangladesh's cost of any future dollar borrowing, since global benchmark rates set the floor for sovereign borrowing costs.

**5. Economists warn Bangladesh Bank's 50-basis-point rate cut to 9.5% will fuel inflation further, as the central bank shifts to quarterly monetary policy reviews.**

A policy rate cut lowers the cost of borrowing across the economy to revive private credit and investment. But it also pumps more money into an economy already facing double-digit input cost inflation from cotton, fuel and gas, which can push consumer prices higher rather than lower. Bangladesh Bank's move to quarterly reviews, from twice yearly, suggests the central bank wants room to reverse course faster if inflation responds badly.

**6. Distressed assets and a growing non-performing loan burden threaten Bangladesh's banking sector, The Daily Star reports.**

Non-performing loans (NPLs) — loans the bank no longer expects to be repaid in full — force banks to set aside provisions that eat directly into profit and capital. Bangladesh's NPL burden is already among the highest in South Asia and concentrated in a handful of large business groups. A rate cut that compresses net interest margin, the spread between lending and deposit rates, arrives at the worst possible time for banks already absorbing credit losses.

**7. Government raises the price of a 12kg LPG cylinder by Tk 70 to Tk 1,598 for August.**

Liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) is imported and priced off international benchmarks, so the government passes cost changes straight to consumers via a monthly formula. A Tk 70 increase on a 12kg cylinder is a direct addition to household cost of living, feeding into headline consumer price inflation. It reflects the same gas-supply shortage that is forcing costly emergency LNG cargo purchases and disrupting industrial production.

**8. Bangladesh's RMG exporters face sharply higher ocean freight rates as the Iran-US-Israel conflict chokes shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.**

Ocean freight is priced in dollars and set by global shipping capacity, and the Strait of Hormuz is a chokepoint a large share of regional tanker and container traffic passes through. Higher freight rates raise the landed cost of RMG exports without raising the price factories charge buyers, so the increase is absorbed directly out of already-thin garment margins. RMG earns roughly 80-85% of Bangladesh's export revenue, so a sustained freight shock is a margin story for the whole export economy.

**9. Global oil majors post windfall profits as the US-Iran conflict enters its sixth month, with Brent near $90 a barrel, even as import-dependent nations like Bangladesh face an energy pinch.** `Brent WTI`

Oil majors profit when crude prices rise because upstream revenue increases faster than costs, while importing countries like Bangladesh pay more for every barrel without producing any of their own. Bangladesh imports nearly all its fuel, so a sustained high oil price widens the subsidy bill state distributors carry and widens the current account deficit. The same roughly $90 Brent price lifting Western energy company earnings is the direct cause of Bangladesh's LNG and LPG cost increases this week.

## How these connect

### The financing squeeze

Foreign aid commitments fall to a 14-year low while debt repayment hits a record $4.49bn (①) → net new foreign financing turns sharply negative → July remittances hit $2.85bn, an 8-year high, but still trail import demand for dollars (②) → reserves hold at $31bn only after absorbing the latest ACU payment, offering a shrinking buffer (③) → simultaneously the US 10-year yield climbs to 4.745%, narrowing the return cushion that could otherwise pull in portfolio capital to fill the gap (④) → every channel that brings dollars into Bangladesh is flat or shrinking at once.

**So what:** This is the structural reason USD/BDT is stuck near record lows and why Bangladesh Bank had to impose an informal cap — no single fix (more remittances, or lower US yields) is enough on its own; the pressure is coming from four directions simultaneously.

### Rate cut meets bank fragility

Bangladesh Bank cuts its policy rate to 9.5% just as the US 10-year yield rises to 4.745% (④→⑤) → domestically, the cut narrows net interest margin for every bank in the system → that lands on top of an already-building non-performing loan pile flagged today as a threat to the banking sector (⑥) → banks face margin compression and credit-quality deterioration at the same time.

**So what:** Expect bank stocks to keep lagging the broader index — this is exactly the mechanism behind the standing predictions that banking sector shares underperform through mid-August.

### One geopolitical shock, two cost lines

**So what:** A single geopolitical trigger is now hitting both sides of Bangladesh's trade account at once — watch RMG margins and energy-cost-sensitive names together, not the energy import bill in isolation.
