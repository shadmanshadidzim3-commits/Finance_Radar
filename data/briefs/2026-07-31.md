## The day in one read

Today was the day Bangladesh's macroeconomic contradictions stopped being theoretical. The central bank cut its policy rate to 9.5% to revive private credit and rescue an economy where development spending has fallen to a 53-year low. It did so while inflation remains high, while Brent is up 23% in a month, while cotton is up 13%, and while three Federal Reserve officials are publicly arguing for a hike. The taka's 1.4% single-session slide and the informal cap on dollar rates that followed are not separate events; they are the immediate, mechanical consequence of easing into a widening rate differential. The domestic picture makes the choice more uncomfortable rather than less. A gas shortage severe enough that the garment lobby is asking to run factories on CNG is a supply constraint, and cheaper credit does not produce gas. The CPD's finding that food prices double between farm and retail says the same thing about inflation: the binding constraint is structural, so monetary easing will reach asset prices long before it reaches output. The equity market obliged, bouncing 1.55% on Tk 995 crore of turnover, but with mutual funds sweeping 21-0 and BEXIMCO pinned at its circuit breaker, this looks like liquidity chasing beta rather than conviction. The number to watch now is the spread between the official and kerb dollar rate. Remittances above $3 billion for five straight months are the only thing holding this together, and an artificially capped official rate is the fastest way to send that flow into the informal market.

## Top 10 stories

**1. Bangladesh Bank cut the policy rate by 50 basis points to 9.5%, its first reduction in years, explicitly to revive private credit growth.**

This is an easing decision taken while inflation is still elevated and the taka is weakening, which is the opposite of the textbook response. It tells you the central bank has shifted its priority from defending prices to defending growth, after development spending collapsed to a 53-year low. Every other Bangladeshi story today has to be read through this lens.

**2. Bangladesh Bank imposed an informal cap on dollar rates as the taka came under renewed pressure, with USD/BDT at 123.58 after a 1.4% single-day move.** `BDT=X`

An informal cap is administrative price control, not a market rate. It is what a central bank does when it wants a weaker policy rate and a stable currency at the same time, which is not sustainable. The immediate risk is that exporters and remitters route dollars outside the banking channel, which drains the very reserves the cap is meant to protect.

<sub>FX · 🇧🇩 direct · confidence: high · The Business Standard · [read](https://www.tbsnews.net/economy)</sub>

**3. A critical gas shortage is halting industrial production nationwide, with BGMEA and BKMEA formally asking for CNG to keep garment factories running.** `TITASGAS`

When the export lobby asks to run factories on vehicle fuel, the energy shortage has stopped being a cost problem and become a capacity problem. Idle capacity means missed shipment deadlines, and in apparel a missed deadline costs the order, not just the margin. This is the most direct threat to export earnings on today's list.

<sub>Energy · 🇧🇩 direct · confidence: high · The Business Standard, Daily Asian Age, Daily Observer · [read](https://www.tbsnews.net/economy)</sub>

**4. Brent crude is up roughly 23% over the past month to about $90, driven by escalating US strikes on Iran and the risk of disruption in the Strait of Hormuz.** `BZ=F CL=F`

Bangladesh imports essentially all of its liquid fuel, so a 23% move in a month is a direct widening of the import bill and the subsidy burden at the same moment the government approved Tk16,089 crore of refined fuel purchases for July-December. It is the external shock sitting underneath the domestic gas crisis and the tariff increases.

**5. Cotton rose 3% on the day and is up over 13% in a month, taking the price to about 82 cents a pound.** `CT=F`

Cotton is the primary raw input for the RMG sector that earns most of Bangladesh's foreign currency. Combined with the gas shortage, exporters are being squeezed from both ends at once: the input costs more and the factory cannot reliably run. Apparel contracts are typically priced in advance, so this compresses margins on orders already booked.

For Bangladesh this widens the gap between where global rates are heading and where Bangladesh Bank just moved, which is precisely the pressure that shows up in the exchange rate.

**7. Remittances exceeded $3 billion for a fifth consecutive month, and Bangladesh aims to resume sending workers to Malaysia in August.**

This is the single strongest support under the reserves and the main reason the taka has depreciated gradually rather than sharply. Resuming Malaysian labour export would extend that support into next year. It also raises the stakes on the informal dollar cap: if the official rate is held below the market rate, this flow is exactly what migrates to informal channels.

**8. The Centre for Policy Dialogue found food prices roughly double between farm gate and retail, with rice doubling and green chilli up 116%.**

A farm-to-retail spread that wide is a distribution and market-power problem, not a supply problem, which means interest rate policy cannot fix it. It explains why headline inflation stays sticky even when harvests are adequate, and it is the strongest argument that today's rate cut will not pass through to the prices households actually face.

**9. Bangladesh's external debt has risen to $78 billion, while annual development spending fell to its lowest level in more than five decades.**

Rising external debt alongside falling development spending is the worst combination: the country is servicing more foreign obligations while building less of the productive capacity that would eventually pay for them. With the taka depreciating, the local-currency cost of that debt rises automatically, without a single new dollar being borrowed.

## How these connect

### The impossible trinity, arriving on schedule
<sub>links #1, #2, #6 · strong</sub>

Bangladesh Bank cut its policy rate to 9.5% → domestic returns on taka assets fall just as the US 10-year yield climbs to 4.72% with three Fed officials pushing for a hike → the interest rate differential moves against the taka → holding dollars becomes more attractive than holding taka → downward pressure on the currency intensifies, and USD/BDT jumps 1.4% in a single session → Bangladesh Bank responds not by reversing the cut but by imposing an informal cap on the dollar rate.

**So what:** A central bank cannot simultaneously set the domestic interest rate, fix the exchange rate, and allow capital to move. Bangladesh Bank has now tried to do all three in one week. The cap is the pressure valve that will fail first: expect the gap between the official and kerb rate to widen, and watch whether remittances start bypassing formal banks. That is the number that tells you whether this policy is holding.

### The RMG margin vice: costlier inputs, no power to use them
<sub>links #3, #5, #4 · strong</sub>

Brent up 23% in a month and a domestic gas shortfall → Petrobangla proposes higher gas and CNG prices while supply is physically rationed → garment factories cannot run reliably, so BGMEA and BKMEA ask to burn CNG instead → simultaneously cotton rises 13% in a month → the sector faces higher input costs and lower usable capacity at the same time → unit costs rise while output falls, on orders whose prices were fixed months ago.

**So what:** This is the mechanism that turns an energy story into an export story into a balance-of-payments story. Apparel buyers do not wait; orders missed in Dhaka get placed in Vietnam or India. If the gas shortage persists into the autumn order cycle, the damage shows up in export receipts with a lag of one to two quarters, which is precisely when the reserve position is most exposed.

### Why the rate cut probably will not reach the shops
<sub>links #1, #8, #3 · plausible</sub>

The rate cut is designed to lower borrowing costs and lift demand → but the CPD finds food prices roughly double between farm and retail, meaning the binding constraint is distribution margins and market power, not the cost of credit → meanwhile the gas shortage constrains physical supply → cheaper money meeting constrained supply and an intermediated retail chain raises prices rather than volumes.

**So what:** Expect the cut to show up in credit growth and asset prices well before it shows up in output, and expect inflation to prove stickier than the central bank is forecasting. For a student watching this play out: this is the clearest real-world case you will get of why monetary policy is a poor tool against supply-side and structural inflation.

### The market voted for the cut, and against the banks
<sub>links #1, #2 · strong</sub>

The rate cut landed → DSE turnover reached Tk 995 crore with 185 advancers against 149 decliners and a turnover-weighted gain of 1.55% → but the banking sector was the exception, falling 0.22% on the day → lower policy rates compress net interest margins, and banks were already the cheapest sector on the exchange at a median P/E of 4.78 → speculative money went elsewhere instead, with mutual funds up 5.07% on 21 advancers and zero decliners, and textiles up 2.65% on the largest share of turnover.

**So what:** The rally is real but its composition is not reassuring. Twenty-one mutual funds rising with none falling is a sweep, not a re-rating, and BEXIMCO hitting exactly +9.95% is the circuit breaker, not a price discovery. Money is chasing beta while the sector with actual earnings visibility trades at under 5x. Treat this bounce as liquidity-driven until blue chips participate.

## Market read

**Dhaka.** A genuine bounce after three losing sessions: Tk 995 crore turnover, 185 advancers to 149 decliners, and a turnover-weighted gain of 1.55%. But the internals are speculative rather than fundamental. Mutual funds rose 5.07% with 21 advancers and not a single decliner, textiles led on the heaviest turnover share at 20.9%, and BEXIMCO closed at exactly +9.95%, which is the circuit-breaker limit rather than a market-clearing price. Banks, the one sector that a rate cut mechanically hurts through margin compression, fell 0.22% and still trade at a median P/E of 4.78 against 25.18 for textiles.

**Global.** A hawkish hold from the Fed with three dissents for a hike pushed the US 10-year to 4.72%, up almost 7% over the month. The energy complex is the dominant story: Brent near $90 and WTI at $85 are both up more than 22% in a month on Iran escalation and Strait of Hormuz risk, though both eased over the past week. Cotton at 82 cents is up 13% in a month. Equities were nearly unchanged in the US, with the Nikkei the outlier at +4%, while the VIX rose 4.5% to 17.9.

**Main transmission channel.** The dominant channel today is the interest rate differential. Bangladesh cut while US yields rose, and that gap transmits into the taka almost immediately, which is why the informal dollar cap appeared within days of the cut. Everything else in the Bangladeshi economy right now runs through the exchange rate, because it converts a global energy and cotton shock directly into domestic inflation and a wider import bill.
