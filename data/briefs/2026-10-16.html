Today's lesson

Concept

Sovereign Risk Premium

When a national government borrows foreign currency by selling bonds on Wall Street, global investors demand an interest rate higher than the baseline risk-free rate, which is set by the US Treasury yield. That additional yield is the sovereign risk premium. It acts as an insurance charge compensating investors for default risk, currency depreciation, and macroeconomic instability.

If the US 10-year Treasury yield is 5.10%, and international investors demand a 600 basis point risk premium for Bangladesh, the government would have to pay an 11.10% interest rate in US dollars. Because servicing dollar debt at double-digit rates quickly drains foreign exchange reserves, developing economies prefer concessional loans from institutions like the IMF and World Bank, which offer below-market borrowing costs.
