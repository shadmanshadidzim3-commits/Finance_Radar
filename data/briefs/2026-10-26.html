Today's lesson

Concept

Sovereign Debt Servicing

When a country borrows money from foreign lenders (like the World Bank or other nations), it must pay back the principal plus interest in foreign currency, usually US dollars. This is called 'debt servicing.' Today's news shows that Bangladesh's debt servicing costs ($1.35 billion) have exceeded the new loans coming in ($662 million).

This is a critical moment for a developing economy. If a country cannot generate enough dollars through exports or remittances to cover these payments, it must dip into its foreign exchange reserves. If reserves run too low, the country may struggle to pay for essential imports like fuel and food, often leading to a currency crisis.

Test yourselfIf a country's debt servicing costs are higher than its new loan inflows and its export earnings are flat, what happens to its foreign exchange reserves? (Answer: They decrease as the country uses them to bridge the gap).

Negative net inflow

When a country pays out more money to foreign lenders and investors than it receives from them in new loans or investments.

Bangladesh's debt repayments ($1.35B) were double its new loan receipts ($662M) in Q1.

It's like a bucket with a hole at the bottom that's bigger than the tap at the top.

Concentration risk

The danger a bank faces when it lends too much money to a single person, company, or industry.

Banks are seeking exemptions to lend even more to City Group, which already owes Tk 26,000 crore.

Don't put all your eggs in one basket—especially if the basket is already cracking.

Budget support

A loan from a foreign country or organization that the government can spend however it needs, rather than on a specific bridge or road.

The government is seeking $1 billion from South Korea to help manage general economic shocks.

It's an 'unrestricted gift card' for the national treasury.

Margin compression

When a company's costs go up but its selling prices stay the same, leaving it with less profit.

Rising gas prices and wage demands are squeezing the profits of garment exporters.

It's the feeling of being squashed between a floor of rising costs and a ceiling of fixed prices.
