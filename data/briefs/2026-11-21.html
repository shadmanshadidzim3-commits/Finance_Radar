Today's lesson

Concept

The Impossible Trinity

The Impossible Trinity (or the Trilemma) states that a country cannot have all three of these at the same time: a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy. Bangladesh has tried to keep the taka stable (fixed-ish) while controlling interest rates (independent policy). However, as dollar demand rose, they began losing foreign reserves.

By moving to a 'fully floating exchange rate,' Bangladesh Bank is choosing to give up control over the currency's price to regain control over its monetary policy and protect its remaining reserves. Today's news is the Trilemma in action: the central bank is finally picking its two sides.

Test yourselfIf Bangladesh moves to a floating exchange rate but wants to keep its own interest rate policy, what must it allow to happen to the taka's value? (Answer: It must allow the value to fluctuate freely based on market demand.)

Jargon decoder

These terms appear in today's brief. Hover any dotted word above to see its meaning without scrolling back.

Floating exchange rate

A system where the value of a currency is set by supply and demand in the market, not by the government.

Bangladesh Bank announced it will move to this system within a month, ending the era of the 'managed' taka.

In a floating system, the central bank is a spectator, not the referee.

Non-performing loan (NPL)

A loan where the borrower has not made scheduled payments for a specific period, usually 90 days.

The NPL ratio in Bangladesh was revealed to have hit 30.6% due to stricter reporting standards.

NPLs are 'zombie' assets—they stay on the books but don't bring in any cash.

Resolution

The process of fixing or closing a failing bank in a way that doesn't crash the rest of the economy.

Padma Bank has been put into a formal resolution process, signaling the end of temporary bailouts.

Resolution is the financial version of intensive care—it's the last stop before a bank is saved or shut.

Fiscal deficit

The gap when a government spends more money than it takes in through taxes and other revenue.

Rising gas prices and low tax-to-GDP ratios are widening the deficit, forcing the government to borrow more.

The deficit is the 'overdraft' of the national budget.
