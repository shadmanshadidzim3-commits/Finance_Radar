Today's lesson

Concept

The interest rate spread and credit rationing

In an ideal banking market, the interest rate spread reflects the administrative cost of intermediation plus a risk premium for borrower default. When default risks rise across an economy—such as during industrial unrest or widespread corporate delinquency—banks should theoretically raise loan rates to compensate. However, when regulators impose artificial spread caps (like Bangladesh Bank's 4% limit), banks cannot price risky loans accurately.

Instead of lending to smaller, viable enterprises at capped rates, banks either breach the cap outright or engage in credit rationing: they restrict lending exclusively to connected conglomerates and starve SMEs. This widens the informal spread, pushes corporate borrowers into distress, and leaves productive firms without working capital.

Interest rate spread

The percentage difference between the loan interest rate a bank charges its borrowers and the deposit interest rate it pays its savers.

Commercial banks in Bangladesh widened this gap toward 6%, defying the central bank's official 4% regulatory cap.

It is the bank's mark-up on money: the higher the spread, the more expensive borrowing is relative to saving.

Bank resolution

A formal regulatory intervention to restructure, recapitalize, or wind down an insolvent bank without causing a systemic panic.

Bangladesh Bank initiated a resolution framework for Padma Bank after years of unmanageable default loans.

It is the financial ICU where failing lenders are either operated on or liquidated to protect everyday depositors.

Single-borrower exposure limit

A regulatory cap that prevents a bank from lending more than a fixed percentage of its capital to any single company or business group.

Bangladesh Bank is tightening corporate lending limits to stop large conglomerates from monopolising bank balance sheets.

The golden rule of credit risk: never allow one corporate borrower to be big enough to break a bank.

Moral hazard

The tendency for people or firms to take excessive risks when they know someone else will absorb their losses.

Allowing defaulted borrowers with court stay orders to access subsidized government stimulus creates a strong incentive to never repay.

When the government buys the insurance, borrowers drive with their eyes closed.
