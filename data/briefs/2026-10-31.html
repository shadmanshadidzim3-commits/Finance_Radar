Today's lesson

Concept

The Crowding-Out Effect in Domestic Credit

When the government offers higher returns on its own debt instruments—such as National Savings Certificates—than commercial banks pay on deposits, rational savers transfer their cash into state paper. This shift drains liquid deposits from commercial banks. To prevent deposits from vanishing entirely, banks must raise their own deposit rates.

This drives up their cost of funds, the interest rate they pay to secure capital. Banks are then forced to raise the lending rates they charge businesses. Consequently, private corporate borrowing and capital investments fall.

The sovereign ends up financing its fiscal deficit by absorbing private savings that would otherwise fund industrial growth.
