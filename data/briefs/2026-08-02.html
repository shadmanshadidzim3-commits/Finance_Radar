The day in one read

Today was a financing day, not a trading day. Nothing dramatic happened on the DSE — the index drifted up on earnings optimism and thin small-cap turnover — but underneath, Bangladesh's external accounts told a harder story. Foreign loan commitments fell to a 14-year low while debt repayment hit a record $4.49 billion. Remittances hit an eight-year high but still can't outrun import demand. Reserves sit at $31 billion after another Asian Clearing Union payment. None of these are shocks on their own; together, they are the financing gap that has been pushing the taka to 122.74 all week.

What is new versus the last two days is that the story has widened from energy to financing. Yesterday it was Brent and LNG cargo costs; the day before it was the rate cut itself. Today confirms the same mechanism from a different angle — Bangladesh Bank cut its policy rate to 9.5% just as the US 10-year yield climbed to 4.745%, narrowing the cushion that used to attract capital into taka assets. Add record debt repayment against collapsing new aid, and every channel that could bring dollars in is either shrinking or barely keeping pace.

Watch USD/BDT against the informal cap Bangladesh Bank imposed this week — if the kerb premium keeps widening while the official rate holds, that is capitulation building, not stability. The number that will confirm or kill this thesis is the next official net reserves print against the $30 billion line.

Today's lesson

Concept

Interest rate differentials and capital flows (uncovered interest parity intuition)

Money crosses borders chasing the best return after adjusting for risk and currency movement. Bangladesh's policy rate is 9.5%, far above the US 10-year Treasury yield of 4.745% — a gap that should pull foreign capital in. But investors weight direction too: US yields are climbing while Bangladesh Bank just cut its rate, so the cushion is compressing, not widening.

The taka has also slid over 1% this month, which eats into that extra taka yield once converted back to dollars. When the currency-adjusted return shrinks, capital that might have flowed in stays in dollars instead — precisely the pressure now showing up in USD/BDT.

Jargon decoder

Net interest margin (NIM)

The gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on deposits — where almost all of a bank's profit comes from.

Bangladesh Bank's rate cut to 9.5% mechanically narrows this gap for every bank in the country.

Ask: does this company earn from lending or borrowing? Banks earn from lending — so a rate cut squeezes them.

Non-performing loan (NPL)

A loan the bank no longer expects to be repaid in full — a bad debt sitting on the balance sheet.

Today's distressed-asset warning on the banking sector shows the NPL pile is still growing even before the rate cut adds margin pressure.

Every NPL looked fine on the day it was made — the risk shows up years later, not immediately.

Asian Clearing Union (ACU) payment

A periodic lump-sum settlement Bangladesh makes with other Asian central banks for two months of accumulated trade with them.

Today's $31 billion reserve figure is reported net of the latest ACU payment — a real, recurring drain on the buffer.

Reserves don't fall smoothly — they step down in chunks whenever an ACU cycle or bond payment comes due.

Interest rate differential

The gap between what you earn holding one country's assets versus another's — a core driver of where global capital flows.

Bangladesh Bank cut its rate to 9.5% while the US 10-year yield rose to 4.745%, narrowing the cushion that compensates investors for taka risk.

Money flows toward wherever the risk-adjusted return is rising, not just wherever the nominal rate is highest.
