Today's lesson

Concept

Negative Real Interest Rates

Interest rates tell you how fast your money grows in nominal terms, but inflation tells you how fast the cost of living grows. The 'real' interest rate is simply the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate. When central banks cut policy rates while inflation remains sticky, commercial deposit rates often drop below the inflation rate.

This creates negative real interest rates. For savers, leaving cash in a bank account becomes a guaranteed loss of purchasing power. For the economy, it drives capital out of formal banking institutions and into physical assets like real estate, gold, or foreign currency.

Negative real interest rate

When the interest rate paid on savings is lower than the rate of inflation, causing cash to lose purchasing power over time.

Commercial banks cut deposit rates below current inflation levels, giving depositors negative real returns on their cash.

If your bank pays 7% interest but prices rise 9%, your savings actually shrink by 2% in real purchasing power.

Capacity utilisation

The percentage of a factory's maximum output capacity that is actually being produced.

Plastic manufacturers reported running at just 30% capacity due to national gas shortages.

A factory running at 30% capacity incurs almost all its fixed overhead costs while generating less than a third of its potential sales.

Provision requirements

Mandatory cash reserves a bank must set aside to cover potential losses from loans that borrowers might default on.

Islami Bank posted a Tk 1,028 crore loss primarily due to massive provisioning against defaulted loans.

Provisions are the financial shock absorber banks must buy today for loans that might go bad tomorrow.

Current account deficit

The net shortage that occurs when a country spends more foreign currency on imports than it earns from exports and remittances.

The taka's sudden depreciation to 123.47 was triggered by persistent current account pressure and strong dollar demand.

It is a nation's trade scorecard — if you buy more from the world than you sell, your currency loses backing.
