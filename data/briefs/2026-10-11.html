Today's lesson

Concept

The Liquidity Trap in Commercial Banking

A liquidity trap occurs when financial institutions hold abundant cash reserves but cannot channel them into productive lending because business demand is moribund and credit risk is elevated. In Bangladesh today, banks hold substantial excess liquidity, yet private-sector credit growth has remained below 5% for six consecutive months. Reluctant to extend fresh corporate credit amidst non-performing loan worries, lenders pile surplus funds into short-term Treasury bills, driving yields down below 8%.

When sovereign yields sink below the cost of maintaining term deposits, bank profit margins contract. Flooding the banking system with liquidity fails to ignite economic activity if private enterprise lacks the confidence or capacity to borrow.

Net interest margin

The gap between what a bank earns on its loans and investments and what it pays depositors in interest.

Nostro account

A bank account that a domestic bank holds in foreign currency at another bank in a foreign country.

Strong Q1 export earnings of $10.59 billion replenish Bangladeshi banks' dollar balances held overseas.

Think of it as your local bank's overseas checking account used to settle international trade.

Loan loss provision

An expense that a bank sets aside from its earnings to cushion against loans that borrowers might fail to repay.

GPH Ispat's plea to restructure Tk 12,000 crore in debt threatens to force participating banks into recording higher credit provisions.

It is money deducted from current profits to build an emergency fund for bad loans.

Current account

The record of a country's net trade in goods, services, and overseas cash gifts like worker remittances.

A 13.1% rise in remittances to $9.37 billion strengthens the inflows that pay for fuel and raw commodity imports.

It shows whether a nation is paying its daily international bills from its own earnings or by borrowing abroad.

Capital adequacy ratio

The ratio of a bank's core capital to its risk-weighted assets, measuring its financial strength against shocks.

Heavy corporate loan restructurings threaten to deplete regulatory capital buffers across vulnerable domestic lenders.

It is a bank's financial shock absorber; the thicker the cushion, the harder it is to fail.
