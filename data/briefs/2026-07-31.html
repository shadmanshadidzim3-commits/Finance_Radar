The day in one read

Today was the day Bangladesh's macroeconomic contradictions stopped being theoretical. The central bank cut its policy rate to 9.5% to revive private credit and rescue an economy where development spending has fallen to a 53-year low. It did so while inflation remains high, while Brent is up 23% in a month, while cotton is up 13%, and while three Federal Reserve officials are publicly arguing for a hike.

The taka's 1.4% single-session slide and the informal cap on dollar rates that followed are not separate events; they are the immediate, mechanical consequence of easing into a widening rate differential. The domestic picture makes the choice more uncomfortable rather than less. A gas shortage severe enough that the garment lobby is asking to run factories on CNG is a supply constraint, and cheaper credit does not produce gas.

The CPD's finding that food prices double between farm and retail says the same thing about inflation: the binding constraint is structural, so monetary easing will reach asset prices long before it reaches output. The equity market obliged, bouncing 1.55% on Tk 995 crore of turnover, but with mutual funds sweeping 21-0 and BEXIMCO pinned at its circuit breaker, this looks like liquidity chasing beta rather than conviction. The number to watch now is the spread between the official and kerb dollar rate.

Remittances above $3 billion for five straight months are the only thing holding this together, and an artificially capped official rate is the fastest way to send that flow into the informal market.

Today's lesson

Concept

The impossible trinity

Every central bank faces three tempting goals: control your own interest rates, keep your exchange rate stable, and let money move freely across your borders. The impossible trinity says you can pick any two. You can never have all three.

Watch it happen today. Bangladesh Bank picked goal one and cut rates to 9.5% to help the economy. But money moves freely, and US rates are rising.

So money started leaving taka for dollars, and the taka fell 1.4% in a session. That broke goal two. Bangladesh Bank's response was an informal cap on the dollar rate, which is an attempt to take goal two back by quietly restricting goal three.

This is not a Bangladeshi mistake. It is a constraint every open economy runs into, and the only real choice is which corner to give up deliberately rather than by accident.

Jargon decoder

These terms appear in today's brief. Hover any dotted word above to see its meaning without scrolling back.

Policy rate

The interest rate the central bank charges commercial banks to borrow. It is the anchor that pulls every other rate in the country up or down.

Bangladesh Bank cut it from 10% to 9.5%, which makes borrowing cheaper everywhere.

It is the price of money. Lower it and money gets cheaper for everyone downstream.

Net interest margin

The gap between what a bank earns on the loans it gives out and what it pays savers for deposits. Almost all bank profit lives in this gap.

A rate cut squeezes this gap, which is why DSE banking stocks fell on a day the market rose.

Banks make money on the spread, not the size. A narrower gap means less profit per taka lent.

Interest rate differential

The difference between interest rates in two countries. Money flows toward wherever it earns more, so this gap drives currencies.

Bangladesh cut while US yields rose, widening the gap against the taka and pushing it down.

Money is a tourist. It goes where it is treated best.

Market breadth

How many stocks rose versus how many fell. It tells you whether a market move was broad conviction or a few big names.

185 rose against 149 that fell, but mutual funds went 21 up and zero down, which signals a sweep rather than genuine buying.

An index tells you what happened. Breadth tells you whether to believe it.

Circuit breaker

A rule that stops a stock's price moving more than a set percentage in one session, on the DSE usually 10%.

BEXIMCO closed at exactly +9.95%, meaning it hit the ceiling. That is not a real market price.

When a stock stops at exactly the limit, the limit set the price, not buyers and sellers.

Current account

A running total of money coming into a country versus going out, mainly from trade and remittances. A deficit means more leaves than arrives.

Costlier oil and cotton widen the outflow, while $3bn of remittances is the main inflow holding it together.

A country's bank statement. Spend more abroad than you earn and your currency eventually pays for it.
