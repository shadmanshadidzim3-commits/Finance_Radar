Today's lesson

Concept

Credit rationing and sovereign crowding out

When a government runs large fiscal deficits, it must borrow heavily from the banking system by issuing treasury bills. Commercial banks have a choice: lend to private businesses which carry default risk, or lend to the government risk-free. When bank balance sheets carry high non-performing loans, risk aversion rises.

Banks choose to invest their Tk 4 lakh crore in excess liquidity into sovereign bonds rather than extending industrial credit. Even if nominal interest rates fall, private companies cannot secure loans because banks are rationing credit in favor of the state. This phenomenon, known as crowding out, depresses private corporate investment.

Jargon decoder

Net interest margin

The gap between what a bank earns lending money and what it pays savers. It is where nearly all bank profit comes from.

BRAC Bank drove its 57% profit surge by expanding this spread while keeping non-performing loans low.

A bank is a retail shop for money; net interest margin is its gross retail markup.

Current account balance

The financial scorecard of a country's trade in goods, services, and incoming money like remittances.

A 16.43% drop in European garment shipments reduces export earnings and widens the deficit.

If the current account is in deficit, the country must borrow dollars or draw down reserves.

Excess liquidity

Cash held by commercial banks above the mandatory legal reserves set by the central bank.

Banks accumulated Tk 4 lakh crore in surplus funds because businesses are hesitant to borrow.

Excess cash in a bank vault is like unsold inventory on a store shelf; it earns minimal yield.

Working capital

The liquid money a business needs every day to buy materials, pay salaries, and keep lights on.

Garment exporters ran out of working capital due to delayed VAT refunds and lower export receipts.

Working capital is the oxygen of a business; a company can be profitable on paper but die without cash.
