Today's lesson

Concept

The Stagflation Trap

Stagflation occurs when an economy experiences slow economic growth alongside high inflation. Standard economic policy works well when growth and inflation move together. When growth is strong and prices heat up, central banks raise interest rates to cool the economy.

When growth stalls and prices fall, central banks cut rates to stimulate investment. In stagflation, monetary tools conflict. Raising interest rates to fight 10.9% inflation makes loans expensive, further suffocating businesses and slowing growth.

But cutting rates to revive 3.2% growth injects cash that accelerates price increases. The only durable escape is expanding domestic supply, such as producing local energy to cut expensive fuel imports.

Concessional financing

Bangladesh is negotiating $10 billion in multi-year assistance from the World Bank to avoid expensive commercial loans.

Cheap development loans designed to build infrastructure, not commercial debt meant to make a bank profit.

Letters of credit

A formal guarantee from a buyer's bank promising that a foreign seller will receive full payment on time.

Import letters of credit opened in September jumped 22% due to rising prices for crude and raw materials.

A bank's written promise that turns a local business order into guaranteed foreign currency trade.

Lender of last resort

A central bank's function of providing emergency cash to solvent financial institutions facing depositor panics.

Bangladesh Bank injected direct liquidity into National Bank so depositors could withdraw their funds.

The financial fire department that supplies emergency cash when no commercial bank will lend.

Moral hazard

The incentive to take reckless risks when someone else absorbs the cost of failure.

The central bank relaxed lending bans for defaulting Beximco units, rewarding non-performing borrowers with credit.

If the government always bails you out, you have no reason to manage risk carefully.
