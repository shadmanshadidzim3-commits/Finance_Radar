Today's lesson

Concept

The Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR)

The Capital Adequacy Ratio is a measurement of a bank's available capital expressed as a percentage of its risk-weighted credit exposures. In simple terms, it is a safety buffer. Banks lend money, and some of those loans go bad.

To prevent the bank from failing when loans aren't repaid, regulators require them to hold a certain amount of their own money (equity) against those risks. Today, Islami Bank's move to increase its authorised capital is a step toward raising more equity. If they issue new shares, they increase their 'Tier 1' capital, which makes the bank more resilient to shocks and improves its CAR.

Test yourselfIf a bank has Tk 10 in capital and Tk 100 in risky loans, what happens to its safety buffer if it issues Tk 5 in new shares? (Answer: Its capital increases to Tk 15, making its buffer stronger against potential loan losses.)

Jargon decoder

These terms appear in today's brief. Hover any dotted word above to see its meaning without scrolling back.

Working capital

The money a business has available to pay for its day-to-day operations, like wages and raw materials.

The NBR account freezes directly threaten the working capital of over 100 firms.

It is the 'oxygen' of a business; without it, even a profitable company can suffocate and die.

Authorised capital

The maximum amount of share capital that a company is legally allowed to issue to shareholders.

Islami Bank is raising this limit to Tk 10,000 crore, likely to prepare for a new share issue.

Think of it as the size of the bucket; it doesn't tell you how much water (money) is in it, only how much it can hold.

Concessional loan

A loan that is granted on terms substantially more generous than market loans, usually with low interest and long grace periods.

The World Bank's $140 million loan for Chattogram water is a classic example of this.

It is 'cheap money' that helps developing nations build infrastructure without a debt trap.

Cost-push inflation

When the prices of goods rise because the cost of making them (like energy or wages) has gone up.

The Tk 475 jump in LPG prices over two months is a major driver of cost-push inflation in Bangladesh.

It is inflation caused by the 'supply' side getting more expensive, not the 'demand' side getting richer.
