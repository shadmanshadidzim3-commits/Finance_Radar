Today's lesson

Concept

The impossible trinity (the Mundell-Fleming trilemma)

A country can only fully have two of these three things at once: a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital and trade in and out, and an independent monetary policy. Bangladesh is trying to have all three right now. Bangladesh Bank wants to defend the taka near a target level (its informal dollar-rate cap is a soft version of a fixed rate).

It also wants to set its own interest rate for domestic reasons — hence yesterday's cut to 9.5% — even as the US moves the other way. And it hasn't closed off trade or capital flows; imports, remittances, and debt payments move freely. Something has to give, and today it was the exchange rate: USD/BDT pushed through the cap anyway, because you cannot hold a currency fixed, keep an open economy, and run independent monetary policy all at once.

Test yourselfIf Bangladesh Bank wants to both cut its policy rate and hold the taka near a fixed level against the dollar, and Bangladesh's trade and remittance flows stay open, what has to give? (Answer: the exchange rate — it keeps sliding through the cap, exactly as it did again today.)

Jargon decoder

These terms appear in today's brief. Hover any dotted word above to see its meaning without scrolling back.

Current account deficit

The gap between the dollars a country pays out (for imports and debt service) and the dollars it takes in (from exports and remittances).

Rising oil and LNG import costs are widening this gap, which is the root cause of today's taka pressure.

Think of it as the country's monthly bank statement in dollars — deficit means more went out than came in, and something has to fill the hole.

Interest rate differential

The gap between what one country's interest rates pay versus another's — here, Bangladesh versus the US.

Bangladesh Bank cut rates yesterday while Fed officials are pushing for hikes, widening the gap in the dollar's favour and pressuring the taka.

Money chases yield — the wider the gap in the dollar's favour, the more reasons global capital has to prefer dollars over taka.

Non-performing loan (NPL) / provisioning

An NPL is a loan not being repaid on schedule. Provisioning is the profit a bank must set aside to cover expected losses on such loans.

Small-loan defaulters rose 2.4 million in a year and S Alam's shutdown raises fresh doubts about large exposures — both point to rising provisioning needs.

A bank's profit is only as good as its loan book — rising NPLs quietly eat reported earnings before you see it in the headline number.

Advance/decline ratio

The number of stocks that rose divided by the number that fell in a session — a simple gauge of how broad a market move really is.

DSEX rose 1.55% but the ratio was only 1.24, meaning the rally was much narrower than the index headline suggests.

The index tells you the average; the advance/decline ratio tells you whether everyone showed up to the party.

Valuation effect (on debt)

A rise in the local-currency cost of foreign debt caused purely by currency depreciation, not by borrowing anything new.

The $78bn external debt gets more expensive to service in taka terms every time the taka depreciates, as it did again today.

You didn't borrow a single new dollar, but if your currency falls, your foreign debt just got heavier in your own money.

Spot price vs. contracted price

A spot price is what you pay for a one-off purchase today; a contracted price is locked in ahead of time, usually cheaper and more predictable.

Today's $22.35/MMBtu LNG cargo is a spot purchase, priced well above typical contract levels, because term suppliers are reportedly not delivering as agreed.

Buying on the spot market is like paying walk-in rates at a hotel instead of your pre-booked price — it works, but it costs you.
