## The day in one read

Today was the day yesterday's mechanical warnings turned into concrete headlines. The dominant force is unchanged from yesterday: Bangladesh Bank cutting its policy rate into a widening global interest rate gap, with the taka absorbing the pressure. USD/BDT pushed to 122.74, extending its slide through the informal cap Bangladesh Bank imposed only a day earlier, while Brent capped its strongest month since March and Dhaka approved another costly emergency LNG cargo at $22.35 per MMBtu. Energy prices, not equities, are writing this story.

What changed versus yesterday is that abstract risk became a real casualty. The gas shortage flagged yesterday as 'halting industrial production nationwide' claimed S Alam Group's entire Karnaphuli operation today — all 11 units shut, over 5,000 workers idled — while NCP's threat to blockade Petrobangla added political risk to the gas price hike meant to fund the subsidy bill. Meanwhile the DSE rallied to 5,900 on press talk of 'earnings optimism,' but an advance/decline ratio of just 1.24, flat blue chips and a 0.22% fall in banks look more like the narrow, speculative pattern flagged yesterday than a genuine re-rating.

Watch two numbers. USD/BDT against the 124 level in an open call — a breach confirms the cap is failing faster than expected. And banking sector turnover share, 8.3% today — if it keeps shrinking while the index climbs, institutional money is voting with its feet on credit risk.

## Top 10 stories

**1. USD/BDT climbs to Tk 122.74, up 0.66% on the day and 1.05% over the month, as dollar demand for fuel and LNG bills keeps outrunning supply.** `USD/BDT`

This is a current account story, not a confidence story: the gap between dollars flowing out (fuel, LNG, debt service) and dollars flowing in (exports, remittances) is widening, and the exchange rate is the release valve. Bangladesh Bank capped the dollar rate informally yesterday; today's move shows the market pushing through that cap, which is what happens when an administrative ceiling meets a genuine imbalance. A weaker taka raises the local-currency cost of every imported input, from cotton to LNG, feeding straight into inflation.

**2. Brent crude closes at $90.12/bbl, capping a 25.9% monthly gain — its strongest month since March.** `BRENT WTI`

Bangladesh pays its fuel and LNG import bill in dollars, so a 26% monthly jump in Brent directly widens the current account deficit — the gap between what the country pays foreigners and what it earns from them. That gap needs financing, mostly by selling reserves or borrowing, and it adds to demand for dollars that is already depreciating the taka (see story 1). If retail fuel prices stay capped by government policy, the state absorbs the difference as a bigger subsidy bill, widening the fiscal deficit too.

**3. Government approves an emergency LNG cargo at $22.35 per MMBtu amid a nationwide gas crisis.**

This is a spot-market purchase — bought cargo by cargo at whatever price is available today — rather than a contracted price locked in months in advance. Spot LNG is typically priced well above contracted cargoes, so each emergency buy directly enlarges the subsidy Petrobangla and the power utilities carry, pressuring the fiscal deficit. It is also paid in dollars, adding to the same reserve and FX demand that is pushing the taka down (story 1), and it is a direct consequence of the gas shortage that shut S Alam's factories today (story 5).

**4. NCP threatens to blockade Petrobangla over a proposed gas price hike, hours after Bangladesh Bank itself warned the hike will fuel inflation.**

**5. S Alam Group shuts all 11 industrial units in Chattogram's Karnaphuli, idling more than 5,000 workers.** `ISLAMIBANK`

This is a direct hit to industrial output and employment, and it is also a credit-quality signal: S Alam is a conglomerate with large, concentrated loan exposure across several banks, including Islami Bank, which fell 2.51% today — one of the day's biggest blue-chip losers even as the broader index rallied. A group-wide production halt raises real questions about debt-servicing capacity on those exposures, adding to a banking sector already carrying a heavy non-performing loan (NPL) burden — loans not being repaid on schedule, which forces banks to set aside profit as a cushion against losses.

**6. Export freight rates more than double and lead times stretch by up to three weeks as shipping lines trim Bangladesh bookings.**

RMG is roughly 80-85% of Bangladesh's export earnings, so anything that raises the cost or unreliability of shipping hits the whole export engine. Buyers typically resist absorbing freight increases, so exporters eat the cost, compressing already-thin margins on top of cotton prices up nearly 10% this month. Longer lead times risk missed delivery windows, which is exactly the kind of reliability problem that pushes buying houses to shift future orders to competing sourcing countries — a slower-moving but more damaging risk than the cost increase itself.

**7. The number of small-loan defaulters rose by 2.4 million in a single year, Bangladesh Bank data show.**

Rising defaults among small, retail-scale borrowers usually show up later in the sector-wide non-performing loan (NPL) ratio — the share of bank loans not being repaid on schedule. Higher NPLs force banks to raise provisioning, a charge against profit set aside to absorb expected losses, which squeezes reported earnings and capital. That works directly against the goal of yesterday's policy rate cut, which was meant to revive private credit growth — banks sitting on rising defaults have less appetite to lend more cheaply.

**8. Bangladesh's external debt has risen to $78 billion, the Finance Minister told Parliament.**

Most external debt is denominated in foreign currency, so every point of taka depreciation (today's 0.66% slide, story 1) mechanically raises the taka cost of servicing it — a valuation effect, meaning the debt burden rises in local-currency terms without a single new dollar being borrowed. Rising debt-service costs compete for the same scarce fiscal space as development spending, which yesterday's note flagged at a 53-year low, and external debt levels are a standard metric IMF programme reviews track closely.

## How these connect

### The energy-FX-inflation loop
<sub>links #1, #2, #3, #4, #8 · strong</sub>

Brent's 26% monthly surge (2) forces Bangladesh into an expensive emergency LNG cargo at $22.35/MMBtu (3) → paid in dollars, this widens the current account deficit and adds to demand pushing USD/BDT to 122.74 (1) → the weaker taka mechanically raises the local-currency cost of servicing the $78bn external debt (8) → the government's proposed gas price hike to fund the subsidy gap triggers a political blockade threat (4), risking delay or dilution right when the fiscal gap needs closing.

**So what:** Every link in this chain pushes the same direction: higher imported cost, weaker currency, tighter fiscal room. Investors should expect Bangladesh Bank to eventually choose between defending the taka (raising rates, reversing yesterday's cut) or letting inflation run — don't expect it to hold both.

### Rate differential squeeze
<sub>links #1, #9 · strong</sub>

Fed officials pushing for US rate hikes (9) widens the gap between what dollar assets and taka assets pay → dollar-denominated assets become relatively more attractive → this reinforces demand for dollars over taka at the exact moment Bangladesh Bank cut its own rate → taka slides further (1).

**So what:** The taka's weakness is not purely a domestic story; it is also a function of global rate expectations. A Fed hike would make Bangladesh Bank's job of holding the informal cap materially harder.

### Credit-quality double bind
<sub>links #5, #7 · plausible</sub>

S Alam Group halts all 11 Karnaphuli units (5), raising doubt about debt-servicing on its large, concentrated bank exposures, at the same moment retail small-loan defaults rose by 2.4 million in a year (7) → both a large single-name shock and a broad-based retail deterioration hit bank asset quality simultaneously.

**So what:** This is a two-front NPL problem — concentrated corporate exposure and diffuse retail stress — landing on a rate cut meant to encourage more lending. Bank equities with known large-conglomerate exposure, like Islami Bank, deserve extra scrutiny.

### RMG's three-front margin squeeze
<sub>links #2, #6 · strong</sub>

Cotton up nearly 10% this month and oil up 26% feed into higher freight and bunker costs → freight rates more than double and lead times stretch three weeks (6) → combined with the gas shortage halting production (story 5's parent theme), RMG exporters face cost, logistics, and reliability pressure at once.

**So what:** Watch the EPB's July export print and any buyer statements on sourcing shifts — this is the mechanism behind two of the desk's open calls on RMG export deceleration and order diversification.

### Rally versus reality
<sub>links #10, #1, #4 · plausible</sub>

DSEX rallies to 5,900 on 'earnings optimism' headlines (10) even as the taka slides through its cap (1) and political risk builds around the gas price hike (4) → the breadth data (A/D ratio 1.24, banks down, blue chips flat) suggest the market is pricing a rosier scenario than the macro data supports.

**So what:** Fade the index headline; the real signal is turnover concentration and blue-chip participation, both of which say this rally has shallow institutional conviction.

**Main transmission channel.** The single most important channel right now is Bangladesh's energy-driven current account deficit — the gap between what it pays for oil and LNG and what it earns from exports and remittances — meeting a policy rate that Bangladesh Bank just cut into a widening gap with US rates. That combination, not global dollar strength, is what is pushing USD/BDT higher and will keep transmitting into imported inflation and debt-servicing costs until the trade gap narrows or the rate cut is reversed.
